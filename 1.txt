# UAV Directional Cruise and Obstacle Avoidance Enabled by Ensemble Algorithms for Large Language Models

###### Abstract

Aiming at the core technical challenges in the tasks of UAV trajectory tracking and obstacle avoidance, this paper proposes an empowered framework integrating a two-stage mechanism-based Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm with a large language model (LLM). The framework adopts a phased decision-making logic: first, the UAV is trained via the PPO algorithm to achieve accurate hovering at any designated point; subsequently, relying on the environmental understanding and decision-making capabilities of the LLM, combined with a custom reward function, the trajectory tracking control and dynamic obstacle avoidance decision-making of the UAV are realized. To verify the effectiveness of the proposed framework, extensive simulation experiments are conducted. The results demonstrate that under the empowerment of this two-stage integrated framework, the accuracy of the UAV's directional movement trajectory tracking within a set time is significantly improved, and the collision rate with obstacles is drastically reduced. Meanwhile, the convergence of the key performance data curves of the algorithm shows distinct advantages compared with the benchmark scheme, which verifies the feasibility and superiority of the proposed method.

**Keywords:** Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), Trajectory Tracking, Obstacle Avoidance, Large Language Model (LLM), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

## I Introduction

Over the past five years, with the exponential growth of computer computing power and the breakthrough development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology, Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), as intelligent platforms integrating perception, decision-making, and execution, have achieved large-scale applications in various fields such as aerial surveying and mapping, power inspection, emergency rescue, and urban logistics [1-3]. Against this backdrop, the cross-integration of the UAV field and Embodied Intelligence has become a research hotspot--Embodied Intelligence emphasizes that agents improve their decision-making capabilities through real-time interaction with the physical environment. This characteristic is highly consistent with the operational requirements of UAVs in dynamically complex scenarios, providing a new technical path for solving the trajectory tracking and obstacle avoidance problems of traditional UAVs [4-6]. Trajectory tracking and obstacle avoidance are core key technologies for UAV autonomous flight, and their performance directly determines the safety of UAV operations and the quality of task completion [7]. Although relevant research has been carried out for decades, traditional methods still face dual bottlenecks in perception and decision-making planning in actual dynamic scenarios, making it difficult to meet the application requirements of high precision and high reliability [8,9]. At the perception level, traditional UAVs rely on a single sensor (such as a monocular camera or lidar) or a simple fusion scheme, which results in insufficient ability to estimate the motion state of dynamic targets (such as moving vehicles, pedestrians, and other UAVs). When dynamic targets are in high-speed movement, random steering, or multi-target interaction states, the perception system is prone to state misjudgment due to data delay and feature extraction failure. Therefore, most traditional studies are forced to limit the experimental scenario to static obstacle environments [10,11].

At the decision-making and planning level, the defects of traditional algorithms in scenario adaptability and robustness are particularly prominent. In terms of trajectory tracking, classical algorithms such as Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) control and Model Predictive Control (MPC) are highly dependent on accurate UAV dynamic models. However, factors such as battery power attenuation, load changes, and airflow interference in actual flight will lead to model mismatch, directly reducing the trajectory tracking accuracy [12,13]. In terms of obstacle avoidance planning, traditional methods such as the Artificial Potential Field Method (APFM) and A* algorithm have inherent defects: APFM is prone to falling into local optimal solutions in scenarios surrounded by multiple obstacles, leading to UAV "stagnation" or "collision"; global planning algorithms such as A* rely on complete environmental maps, resulting in extremely poor real-time performance in unknown dynamic environments, which cannot meet the millisecond-level obstacle avoidance decision-making requirements [14,15]. The above problems are coupled with each other, making it a technical bottleneck in traditional research to realize high-precision trajectory tracking and high-reliability obstacle avoidance simultaneously.

To break through the above limitations, scholars at home and abroad have carried out extensive explorations, such as improving perception stability through multi-sensor fusion [16] and optimizing path planning based on improved heuristic algorithms [17]. However, these methods mostly focus on local improvements in a single link and fail to solve the collaborative optimization problem of "perception-decision" from the system level. In response to this situation, this paper proposes an autonomous UAV control framework empowered by the collaboration of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm and Large Language Model (LLM). By designing a new reward function and a two-stage optimization mechanism, the framework achieves the simultaneous improvement of trajectory tracking accuracy and obstacle avoidance reliability. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1. A collaborative decision-making mechanism of LLM-PPO is proposed: the environmental understanding and logical reasoning capabilities of LLM are introduced into the UAV control process. LLM parses the semantic information of the dynamic environment (such as "fast-approaching vehicles" and "temporarily appearing pedestrians") to provide high-level decision guidance for the PPO algorithm, which significantly improves the intelligence level of the trajectory tracking process and the obstacle avoidance success rate.

2. A two-stage progressive optimization experimental mechanism is designed: in the first stage, the PPO algorithm is used to specially train the UAV's fixed-point hovering ability to build a stable trajectory control foundation; in the second stage, the LLM combines a customized reward function (integrating position error, obstacle safety distance, motion smoothness and other indicators) to dominate the obstacle avoidance decision-making in the directional movement process. The problem is solved in sequence and targetedly, which improves the robustness and fault tolerance of the entire process and enhances the reliability of practical applications.

3. A large number of simulation results verify the effectiveness of the framework: in the dynamic multi-obstacle scenario, the proposed framework reduces the average position error of UAV trajectory tracking by 42.3%, reduces the obstacle collision rate to 1.2%, and significantly improves the convergence speed of both trajectory tracking accuracy and obstacle avoidance decision-making, verifying its application value in complex environments.

## II Methodology

### _Problem Formulation_

We consider a quadrotor modeled as a 6-DoF rigid body with state vector:

$$\mathbf{x}=[\mathbf{p}^{T},\mathbf{v}^{T},\mathbf{q}^{T},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{13},$$

where $\mathbf{p}=[p_x, p_y, p_z]^T\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is position in world frame, $\mathbf{v}=[v_x, v_y, v_z]^T\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is linear velocity, $\mathbf{q}=[q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z]^T\in SO(3)$ is attitude quaternion with $\|\mathbf{q}\|=1$, and $\boldsymbol{\omega}=[\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z]^T\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is angular velocity in body frame. 

The continuous-time quadrotor dynamics is governed by Newton-Euler equations:

$$\mathbf{\dot{p}} = \mathbf{v},$$

$$m\mathbf{\dot{v}} = mg\mathbf{e}_z + \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q})T\mathbf{e}_z,$$

$$\mathbf{\dot{q}} = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}\otimes[\boldsymbol{\omega}^T, 0]^T,$$

$$\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}} = \boldsymbol{\tau} - \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\omega}),$$

where $m=0.027$ kg is the quadrotor mass, $g=9.81$ m/s$^2$ is gravitational acceleration, $\mathbf{e}_z=[0,0,1]^T$ is the unit vector in z-direction, $\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q})\in SO(3)$ is the rotation matrix from body to world frame, $T\in\mathbb{R}$ is total thrust, $\boldsymbol{\tau}=[\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]^T\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is control torque, and $\mathbf{J}=\text{diag}(1.4\times10^{-5}, 1.4\times10^{-5}, 2.17\times10^{-5})$ kg·m$^2$ is the inertia matrix.

The discrete-time dynamics with sampling time $T_s = 0.02$ s (50 Hz) is obtained via 4th-order Runge-Kutta integration:

$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})+\mathbf{w}_{k},$$

where $\mathbf{u}=[T, \boldsymbol{\tau}]^T\in\mathbb{R}^4$ is the control input, and $\mathbf{w}_k\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ with $\sigma=0.1$ m/s$^2$ represents bounded Gaussian disturbance modeling aerodynamic drag and wind gust.

### _Two-Stage Framework Design_

The proposed hybrid-intelligence framework decomposes the mission into two sequential stages: low-level control acquisition and high-level planning augmentation.

#### II-B1 Stage 1: PPO Training for Hover Capability

A PPO agent is first trained to hover accurately at an arbitrary 3-D target. This stage provides a stable low-level controller and reduces the complexity of subsequent navigation tasks. The observation space is simplified to 22 dimensions, and the action space is enlarged to $\pm 50\%$ RPM to ensure sufficient control authority.

**Observation Space Design:** The 22-dimensional observation vector $\mathbf{o}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{22}$ is constructed as:

$$\mathbf{o}_t = [\mathbf{p}_t^T, \mathbf{q}_t^T, \mathbf{v}_t^T, \boldsymbol{\omega}_t^T, (\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} - \mathbf{p}_t)^T, \text{RPM}_t^T]^T,$$

where the components are:
- $\mathbf{p}_t\in\mathbb{R}^3$: current position (indices 0-2)
- $\mathbf{q}_t\in\mathbb{R}^4$: attitude quaternion (indices 3-6)
- $\mathbf{v}_t\in\mathbb{R}^3$: linear velocity (indices 7-9)
- $\boldsymbol{\omega}_t\in\mathbb{R}^3$: angular velocity (indices 10-12)
- $(\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} - \mathbf{p}_t)\in\mathbb{R}^3$: relative goal position (indices 13-15)
- $\text{RPM}_t\in\mathbb{R}^4$: normalized motor speeds (indices 16-19)
- Reserved indices 20-21 for future use

**Action Space Design:** The action $\mathbf{a}_t\in[-1,1]^4$ is mapped to motor RPM commands via:

$$\text{RPM}_i = \text{RPM}_{\text{base}} \times (1 + 0.5 \cdot \mathbf{a}_t[i]), \quad i\in\{0,1,2,3\},$$

where $\text{RPM}_{\text{base}}=16000$ is the hover RPM. This provides $\pm 50\%$ modulation range: $\text{RPM}_i\in[8000, 24000]$, yielding 3× larger control authority than SAC/TD3's $\pm 5\%$ range.

**PPO Objective Function:** The policy $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t|\mathbf{o}_t)$ and value function $V_\phi(\mathbf{o}_t)$ are optimized via the clipped surrogate objective:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right],$$

where $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t|\mathbf{o}_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\mathbf{a}_t|\mathbf{o}_t)}$ is the probability ratio, $\hat{A}_t$ is the Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) with $\lambda=0.95$:

$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}, \quad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(\mathbf{o}_{t+1}) - V_\phi(\mathbf{o}_t),$$

and $\epsilon=0.2$ is the clipping threshold. The value loss is:

$$L^{V}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[(V_\phi(\mathbf{o}_t) - R_t)^2\right],$$

where $R_t = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma^l r_{t+l}$ is the discounted return with $\gamma=0.99$.

Training follows a curriculum from 1-waypoint (static) to 2-waypoint (static + obstacle) scenarios, totalling 2M steps with 8 parallel environments. Upon convergence, the PPO policy is frozen and exported as the low-level actuator for Stage 2.

#### II-B2 Stage 2: LLM Planning + Virtual Targets

After Stage 1 convergence, *all* algorithms (PPO, SAC, TD3) share an identical high-level LLM planner. The workflow is:

1. **State acquisition**: current pose and final goal;
2. **LLM geometric planning**: generate a 0.2 m-spaced waypoint sequence with obstacle-avoidance strategy (vertical / lateral / three-segment / high-altitude);
3. **Virtual-target injection**: dynamically overwrite `obs[vt_induces]` with the next waypoint vector;
4. **Short-range tracking**: RL agent follows the virtual target within 0.2 m tolerance;
5. **Loop**: repeat until the final goal is reached.

This design decouples global planning (LLM) from local execution (RL), improves generalisation, and maintains zero-collision flight even when $h(\mathbf{x})<0$.

**Virtual Target Technology:** The core innovation lies in dynamically decomposing long-range navigation into short-range sub-tasks. Given the current position $\mathbf{p}_t$ and the LLM-generated waypoint sequence $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{w}_M\}$, the virtual target $\mathbf{p}_{\text{vt}}$ is selected as:

$$\mathbf{p}_{\text{vt}} = \mathbf{w}_j, \quad j = \min\{i \mid \|\mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{w}_i\| > 0.15 \text{ m}\},$$

The observation vector is then modified as:

$$\mathbf{o}_{\text{vt}}[\text{vt\_indices}] = \mathbf{p}_{\text{vt}} - \mathbf{p}_t,$$

where `vt_indices` are predefined dimensions (e.g., indices 16-18 in the 22-D observation space for PPO). This operation aligns the test distribution with the training distribution: the RL policy, trained only on short-range hovering (0.2 m), now perceives each waypoint as an immediate goal, enabling seamless execution of long-range trajectories (1-2 m) without retraining.

**LLM Geometric Planning:** The LLM planner selects an obstacle-avoidance strategy $\pi_{\text{plan}}$ from a policy set $\Pi = \{\pi_{\text{vertical}}, \pi_{\text{lateral}}, \pi_{\text{3-seg}}, \pi_{\text{high-alt}}\}$ based on obstacle configuration:

$$\pi_{\text{plan}} = \arg\min_{\pi \in \Pi} L_{\text{path}}(\pi),$$

where $L_{\text{path}}(\pi)$ is the path length computed as:

$$L_{\text{path}}(\pi) = \sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\|\mathbf{w}_{i+1} - \mathbf{w}_i\|,$$

with $M$ being the total number of waypoints. The four strategies are:

1. **Vertical Strategy ($\pi_{\text{vertical}}$):** Climbs vertically to clear obstacles, then moves horizontally:
   $$\mathbf{w}_i = \begin{cases}
   \mathbf{p}_t + i \cdot 0.2\hat{\mathbf{z}}, & i = 1,\ldots,\lceil h_{\text{clearance}}/0.2\rceil \\
   \mathbf{w}_{i-1} + 0.2\frac{\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} - \mathbf{w}_{i-1}}{\|\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} - \mathbf{w}_{i-1}\|}, & \text{otherwise}
   \end{cases}$$
   where $h_{\text{clearance}} = z_{\text{obs}} + r_{\text{obs}} + 0.3$ m ensures obstacle clearance with 0.3 m safety margin.

2. **Lateral Strategy ($\pi_{\text{lateral}}$):** Detours horizontally around obstacles:
   $$\mathbf{w}_{\text{detour}} = \mathbf{p}_{\text{obs}} + (r_{\text{obs}} + 0.5)\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\perp},$$
   where $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\perp}$ is the unit normal perpendicular to the line connecting $\mathbf{p}_t$ and $\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}$.

3. **Three-Segment Strategy ($\pi_{\text{3-seg}}$):** Combines vertical and horizontal maneuvers in three phases.

4. **High-Altitude Strategy ($\pi_{\text{high-alt}}$):** Climbs to maximum height (2.8 m) for dense obstacle fields.

The LLM receives a natural language prompt:
```
"Given current position {p_t}, goal {p_goal}, and obstacles {obs_list}, 
generate a waypoint sequence with 0.2m spacing that avoids collisions."
```
and outputs a JSON-formatted waypoint list:
```json
{"strategy": "vertical", "waypoints": [[x1,y1,z1], [x2,y2,z2], ...]}
```

#### II-B3 Alternative RL Algorithms: SAC and TD3

While PPO serves as the primary low-level controller, we also evaluate Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) to validate the generality of the two-stage framework.

**Soft Actor-Critic (SAC):** SAC maximizes the entropy-regularized objective:

$$J(\pi) = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)\sim\rho_\pi}\left[r(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t) + \alpha\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_t))\right],$$

where $\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|\mathbf{s})) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\pi}[\log\pi(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})]$ is the policy entropy, and $\alpha$ is the temperature parameter automatically tuned to maintain target entropy $\mathcal{H}_{\text{target}} = -\dim(\mathcal{A})$.

The twin Q-functions $Q_{\phi_1}, Q_{\phi_2}$ are trained to minimize the soft Bellman residual:

$$L_Q(\phi_i) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(Q_{\phi_i}(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t) - y_t\right)^2\right],$$

where the target is:

$$y_t = r_t + \gamma\left(\min_{i=1,2}Q_{\bar{\phi}_i}(\mathbf{s}_{t+1},\mathbf{a}_{t+1}) - \alpha\log\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_{t+1}|\mathbf{s}_{t+1})\right),$$

with $\mathbf{a}_{t+1}\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_{t+1})$ sampled from the current policy, and $\bar{\phi}_i$ denoting target network parameters updated via:

$$\bar{\phi}_i \leftarrow \tau\phi_i + (1-\tau)\bar{\phi}_i, \quad \tau=0.005.$$

The policy is updated to maximize the expected Q-value minus entropy:

$$L_\pi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}_t\sim\mathcal{D},\mathbf{a}_t\sim\pi_\theta}\left[\alpha\log\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}_t|\mathbf{s}_t) - Q_{\phi_1}(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)\right].$$

**Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3):** TD3 builds on DDPG with three key improvements:

1. **Twin Q-networks:** Mitigate overestimation by taking the minimum:
   $$y_t = r_t + \gamma\min_{i=1,2}Q_{\bar{\phi}_i}(\mathbf{s}_{t+1}, \pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t+1})).$$

2. **Target policy smoothing:** Add noise to target actions for robustness:
   $$\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{t+1} = \pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t+1}) + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon\sim\text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\sigma}), -c, c),$$
   where $\tilde{\sigma}=0.2$ and $c=0.5$ in our implementation.

3. **Delayed policy updates:** Update policy less frequently (every $d=2$ critic updates) to reduce variance:
   $$L_\pi(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}_t\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[Q_{\phi_1}(\mathbf{s}_t, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t))\right].$$

Both SAC and TD3 use 75-D observation space (vs. PPO's 22-D) and smaller action range (±5% RPM vs. ±50%) to ensure fair comparison of algorithm capabilities rather than hyperparameter tuning.

The complete training and deployment pipeline is formalized in the following algorithms:

```

2:     for mini-batch B ⊂ D do

4:         r(θ) ← π_θ(a|o) / π_θ_old(a|o)
5:         
6:         // Clipped surrogate objective
7:         L^CLIP ← min(r(θ)·Â, clip(r(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε)·Â)
8:         
9:         // Value function loss

16:    
17:    // Check waypoint reaching condition

19:        current_waypoint_idx ← current_waypoint_idx + 1

21:    
22:    // Check collision

25:    end if

27:    t ← t + 1
28:    p_t ← p_{t+1}
29: end while
30:
31: // Check final goal reaching
32: if ||p_t - p_goal|| < 0.5 then
33:     return SUCCESS, τ
34: else
35:     return TIMEOUT, τ
36: end if
```

**Algorithm 5: SAC Training Loop**
```
Input: Replay buffer D, Q-networks Q_φ₁, Q_φ₂, policy π_θ, temperature α
Output: Updated θ, φ₁, φ₂, α

1: Initialize target networks: φ̄₁ ← φ₁, φ̄₂ ← φ₂
2: Initialize temperature: α ← INITIAL_TEMPERATURE
3:
4: for iteration = 1 to N do
5:     // Collect experience
6:     s_t ← ENV.reset()
7:     a_t ~ π_θ(·|s_t)
8:     s_{t+1}, r_t ← ENV.step(a_t)
9:     D.store(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})
10:    
11:    // Sample mini-batch
12:    B ← SAMPLE(D, batch_size=256)
13:    
14:    // Compute target
15:    a_{t+1} ~ π_θ(·|s_{t+1})
16:    y ← r + γ(min(Q_φ̄₁(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1}), Q_φ̄₂(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1})) - α·log π_θ(a_{t+1}|s_{t+1}))
17:    
18:    // Update Q-functions
19:    L_Q ← Σ(Q_φᵢ(s, a) - y)²  for i ∈ {1,2}
20:    φᵢ ← φᵢ - β∇_φᵢ L_Q
21:    
22:    // Update policy
23:    L_π ← E[α·log π_θ(a|s) - Q_φ₁(s, a)]
24:    θ ← θ - β∇_θ L_π
25:    
26:    // Update temperature
27:    L_α ← -α(log π_θ(a|s) + H_target)
28:    α ← α - β∇_α L_α
29:    
30:    // Soft update targets
31:    φ̄ᵢ ← τφᵢ + (1-τ)φ̄ᵢ  for i ∈ {1,2}
32: end for
33: return θ, φ₁, φ₂, α
```

**Algorithm 6: TD3 Training Loop**
```
Input: Replay buffer D, Q-networks Q_φ₁, Q_φ₂, policy π_θ, noise σ
Output: Updated θ, φ₁, φ₂

1: Initialize target networks: θ̄ ← θ, φ̄₁ ← φ₁, φ̄₂ ← φ₂
2: policy_delay ← 2
3:
4: for iteration = 1 to N do
5:     // Collect experience with exploration noise
6:     s_t ← ENV.reset()
7:     a_t ← π_θ(s_t) + ε, ε ~ N(0, σ)
8:     s_{t+1}, r_t ← ENV.step(a_t)
9:     D.store(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})
10:    
11:    // Sample mini-batch
12:    B ← SAMPLE(D, batch_size=256)
13:    
14:    // Target policy smoothing
15:    ã ← π_θ̄(s_{t+1}) + clip(ε, -c, c), ε ~ N(0, σ̃)
16:    
17:    // Compute target with double Q-learning
18:    y ← r + γ·min(Q_φ̄₁(s_{t+1}, ã), Q_φ̄₂(s_{t+1}, ã))
19:    
20:    // Update both Q-functions
21:    L_Q ← Σ(Q_φᵢ(s, a) - y)²  for i ∈ {1,2}
22:    φᵢ ← φᵢ - β∇_φᵢ L_Q
23:    
24:    // Delayed policy update
25:    if iteration mod policy_delay == 0 then
26:        L_π ← -E[Q_φ₁(s, π_θ(s))]
27:        θ ← θ - β∇_θ L_π
28:        
29:        // Soft update all targets
30:        θ̄ ← τθ + (1-τ)θ̄
31:        φ̄ᵢ ← τφᵢ + (1-τ)φ̄ᵢ  for i ∈ {1,2}
32:    end if
33: end for
34: return θ, φ₁, φ₂
```

### _Robust MPC with Control Barrier Functions_

Our RMPC solves the following finite-horizon optimization at each step:

$$
\min_{\mathbf{U}_{k}} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left(\|\mathbf{x}_{k+i}-\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{ref}}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{k+i}\|_{\mathbf{R}}^{2}\right)
$$
subject to:
$$
\mathbf{x}_{k+i+1}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k+i},\mathbf{u}_{k+i}),
$$
$$
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}_{k+i})\geq 0,\quad \forall i\in[0,N-1],
$$
$$
\mathbf{u}_{\min}\leq \mathbf{u}_{k+i}\leq \mathbf{u}_{\max},
$$

where $N=20$ is the prediction horizon, $\mathbf{Q}=\textrm{diag}(10,10,10,1,\ldots)$, $\mathbf{R}=\textrm{diag}(1,1,1,1)$.

The CBF constraint ensures safety via:

$$\dot{h}(\mathbf{x})+\gamma h(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\quad \gamma=10,$$

with the barrier function explicitly defined as

$$h(\mathbf{x})=d_{\textrm{obs}}(\mathbf{x})-d_{\textrm{safe}},\quad d_{\textrm{safe}}=0.50\,\mathrm{m},$$

where $d_{\textrm{obs}}(\mathbf{x})=\min_{i\in\mathcal{O}}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o}_{i}\|$ is the minimum distance from the UAV to any obstacle centre, and $\mathcal{O}$ denotes the obstacle set.

Experimental results show that TD3+LLM achieves a typical minimum $d_{\textrm{obs}}=0.341\,\mathrm{m}$, yielding $h(\mathbf{x})=-0.159\,\mathrm{m}< 0$ (slightly inside the safety margin), yet the collision rate remains 0 %, demonstrating high control precision.

### _Solver Implementation_

We use ACADO with QP-OASES to solve the optimization problem in real time. The solver is warm-started with the previous solution, achieving an average 12.3 ms computation time on Jetson Orin NX.

### _Curriculum Learning Mechanism_

To mitigate the sparse-reward problem in long-horizon navigation, a stage-wise curriculum 1→2→5 waypoints is adopted. Each stage uses the *same* low-level policy but increases the number of intermediate targets and obstacle density. Knowledge is transferred by initialising the next stage with the converged weights of the previous one. Table I summarises the training schedule.

The curriculum learning strategy progressively increases task difficulty while maintaining high success rates. The parameter transfer mechanism is formulated as:

$$\theta_{s+1}^{0} = \theta_{s}^{*}, \quad s \in \{0, 1, 2\},$$

where $\theta_{s}^{*}$ denotes the converged policy parameters at stage $s$, and $\theta_{s+1}^{0}$ is the initialization for stage $s+1$. This warm-start strategy accelerates convergence and prevents catastrophic forgetting.

The effectiveness of curriculum learning is quantified by comparing the final success rate under two training paradigms:

$$\Delta_{\text{curriculum}} = SR_{\text{1→2→5}} - SR_{\text{direct-5}},$$

where $SR_{\text{1→2→5}} = 96.7\%$ represents the curriculum-trained model, and $SR_{\text{direct-5}} \approx 0\text{-}20\%$ represents direct training on 5-waypoint tasks without curriculum. This yields $\Delta_{\text{curriculum}} \geq 76.7\%$, demonstrating the critical importance of gradual task progression.

**TABLE I: CURRICULUM SCHEDULE (PPO-V5 EXAMPLE)**

| Stage | Waypoints | Obstacles | Steps | Success Rate | Convergence Time |
| ----- | --------- | --------- | ----- | ------------ | ---------------- |
| 0     | 1         | 0         | 500K  | 95%          | ~25 min          |
| 1     | 1         | 2         | 1.0M  | 100%         | ~50 min          |
| 2     | 2         | 2         | 1.5M  | 96.7%        | ~75 min          |
| 4     | 5         | 2         | 1.92M | 96.7%        | ~96 min          |
| **Total** | **Progressive** | **Increasing** | **~6.5M** | **96.7%** | **~4 hours** |

The convergence criterion at each stage is:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\text{rollout}}[R_{\text{total}}] \geq R_{\text{threshold}}^{(s)},$$

where $R_{\text{total}} = \sum_{t=0}^{T}R_t$ is the episode return and thresholds are: $R_{\text{threshold}}^{(0)}=500$, $R_{\text{threshold}}^{(1)}=800$, $R_{\text{threshold}}^{(2)}=1000$, $R_{\text{threshold}}^{(4)}=1200$.

## III. EXPERIMENTS

### A. Experimental Workflow

The complete experimental pipeline consists of four sequential phases:

**Phase 1: Environment Setup and Validation**
1. Configure PyBullet physics engine with quadrotor model (CF2X parameters)
2. Implement NavigationAviaryWithObstacles75D environment
3. Validate dynamics via hovering tests: $\|\mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{p}_{\text{hover}}\| < 0.05$ m

**Phase 2: Stage-1 Training (RL Low-Level Control)**
1. Initialize PPO/SAC/TD3 agents with random weights $\theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.01)$
2. Execute curriculum learning schedule (Table I)
3. Monitor convergence via episodic return: $\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{total}}] \geq R_{\text{threshold}}$
4. Save converged policies: $\theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[R_{\text{total}}]$

**Phase 3: Stage-2 Integration (LLM High-Level Planning)**
1. Deploy LLM planner (GPT-4-turbo via OpenAI API)
2. Implement virtual target injection pipeline
3. Test on predefined environments (Env1-3)
4. Collect performance metrics (SR, RMSE, CR)

**Phase 4: Ablation and Analysis**
1. Remove components systematically (LLM, virtual targets, curriculum)
2. Re-evaluate on test set (30 episodes per configuration)
3. Compute contribution deltas: $\Delta_{\text{component}} = \text{SR}_{\text{full}} - \text{SR}_{\text{ablated}}$
4. Statistical testing: paired t-test with $\alpha=0.05$

### B. Implementation Details

All simulations were conducted in PyBullet using the in-house environment NavigationAviaryWithObstacles75D. The flight zone is a $10 \times 10 \times 3  \text{m}^3$ arena containing two spherical obstacles whose positions and radii are randomized. Key environmental parameters are listed in Table II.

**TABLE II: PYBULLET ENVIRONMENT CONFIGURATION**

| Parameter         | Value                                     |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Platform          | PyBullet + NavigationAviaryWithObstacles  |
| Obstacles         | 2 spherical (position/radius variable)    |
| Observation dim.  | PPO 22-D, SAC/TD3 75-D                    |
| Action range      | PPO $\pm 50\%$ RPM, SAC/TD3 $\pm 5\%$ RPM |
| Max. step/episode | 2000                                      |
| Sampling time     | 0.025 s                                   |

**Obstacle Randomization:** At the start of each episode, obstacle parameters are sampled from:

$$\mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},i} \sim \mathcal{U}([1,9]\times[1,9]\times[0.5,2.5]), \quad r_{\text{obs},i} \sim \mathcal{U}(0.3, 0.6),$$

where $\mathcal{U}$ denotes uniform distribution. This ensures diversity in obstacle layouts and prevents overfitting.

**Goal Sampling:** The goal position is randomly generated while maintaining a minimum distance from obstacles:

$$\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} \sim \mathcal{U}([1,9]\times[1,9]\times[0.5,2.5]), \quad \text{s.t. } \min_i\|\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} - \mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},i}\| \geq r_{\text{obs},i} + 1.0 \text{ m}.$$

Training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX-4090 GPU with PyTorch 2.0. Identical hyper-parameters (Table III) were adopted for all three RL algorithms to ensure fair comparison.

**TABLE III: RL TRAINING HYPER-PARAMETERS**

| Algorithm | Total Steps     | Batch | Buffer          | LR   | $\gamma$ | Noise | Policy Network | Value Network |
| --------- | --------------- | ----- | --------------- | ---- | -------- | ----- | -------------- | ------------- |
| PPO       | $2 \times 10^6$ | 2048  | -               | 3e-4 | 0.99     | -     | [64, 64]       | [64, 64]      |
| SAC       | $5 \times 10^5$ | 256   | $1 \times 10^6$ | 3e-4 | 0.99     | -     | [256, 256]     | [256, 256]    |
| TD3       | $5 \times 10^5$ | 256   | $2 \times 10^3$ | 3e-4 | 0.99     | 0.2   | [256, 256]     | [256, 256]    |

**Algorithm-Specific Details:**

1. **PPO:** Uses clipped objective with clip ratio $\epsilon=0.2$, GAE with $\lambda=0.95$, entropy coefficient $c_{\text{ent}}=0.01$, and 10 epochs per update. The policy and value networks are 2-layer MLPs with ReLU activation.

2. **SAC:** Employs twin Q-networks to mitigate overestimation bias, automatic temperature tuning with target entropy $\mathcal{H}_{\text{target}}=-\dim(\mathcal{A})=-4$, and soft update with $\tau=0.005$:
   $$\theta_{\text{target}} \leftarrow \tau\theta + (1-\tau)\theta_{\text{target}}.$$

3. **TD3:** Implements delayed policy updates (every 2 critic updates), target policy smoothing with noise $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.2)$ clipped to $[-0.5, 0.5]$, and twin Q-networks.

The reward function (identical for all agents) is

$$R_t = -\alpha \left\| \mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} \right\| - \beta \max(0, d_{\text{safe}} - d_{\text{obs}}(t)) - \gamma \left\| \mathbf{u}_t \right\|^2,$$

where the three terms penalize:
1. **Position error** ($\alpha=10$): Euclidean distance to goal encourages convergence
2. **Collision risk** ($\beta=50$): Activated when $d_{\text{obs}}(t) < d_{\text{safe}}=0.50$ m, providing strong repulsive signal
3. **Control effort** ($\gamma=0.01$): Squared norm of control input for smoothness, preventing oscillations

The observation-obstacle distance $d_{\text{obs}}(t)$ is computed as:

$$d_{\text{obs}}(t) = \min_{i=1}^{N_{\text{obs}}}\left(\|\mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},i}\| - r_{\text{obs},i}\right),$$

where $N_{\text{obs}}=2$ is the number of obstacles, $\mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},i}$ and $r_{\text{obs},i}$ are the center and radius of obstacle $i$. When $d_{\text{obs}}(t) \leq 0$, a collision occurs.

**Reward Shaping Analysis:** The weight ratio $\beta/\alpha = 5$ ensures that collision avoidance dominates trajectory tracking when obstacles are nearby. Specifically, at the safety boundary ($d_{\text{obs}} = d_{\text{safe}} = 0.5$ m), the collision penalty equals:

$$R_{\text{collision}} = -\beta \cdot d_{\text{safe}} = -50 \times 0.5 = -25,$$

which is equivalent to being $2.5$ m away from the goal ($-\alpha \times 2.5 = -25$). This enforces safety-critical behavior.

The control effort term prevents high-frequency control switches. The expected control cost over a trajectory is:

$$\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{control}}] = -\gamma\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^T\|\mathbf{u}_t\|^2\right] \approx -0.01 \times 200 \times 16 = -32,$$

assuming $\|\mathbf{u}_t\|^2 \approx 16$ (4 motors at $\pm 2$ normalized units) and $T=200$ steps, providing mild regularization without overshadowing the main objectives.

**Episode Termination Criteria:**
- **Success:** $\|\mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}\| < 0.5$ m and $d_{\text{obs}}(t) > 0$, awarding bonus $R_{\text{bonus}} = +100$
- **Failure (Collision):** $d_{\text{obs}}(t) \leq 0$, penalizing $R_{\text{penalty}} = -200$
- **Failure (Out-of-Bounds):** $\|\mathbf{p}_t\| > 5$ m or $z_t < 0$ or $z_t > 3$ m
- **Timeout:** $t > 2000$ steps ($40$ s at 50 Hz)

The success rate is computed as:

$$\text{SR} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{eval}}}\mathbb{1}[\text{episode}_j = \text{success}],$$

where $N_{\text{eval}}=30$ episodes per test environment, ensuring statistical reliability with standard error $\sigma_{\text{SR}} \approx \sqrt{\text{SR}(1-\text{SR})/30} \leq 0.091$.

Evaluation and ablation studies were executed via the scripts listed in Table IV. Reported metrics include success rate, mean final distance, mean steps, mean reward, collision rate, and timeout rate.

**TABLE IV: EVALUATION AND ABLATION SCRIPTS**

| Script                   | Purpose                  |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------ |
| test_ppo_llm.py          | PPO+LLM full test        |
| test_sac_75d.py          | SAC+LLM full test        |
| test_id3_llm_detailed.py | TD3+LLM full test        |
| ablation_llm_module.py   | LLM planner ablation     |
| aggregate_llm_results.py | Batch result aggregation |

**Evaluation Metrics:** For each test configuration, we compute:

1. **Success Rate (SR):** Percentage of episodes reaching goal without collision
   $$\text{SR} = \frac{N_{\text{success}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \times 100\%.$$

2. **Collision Rate (CR):** Percentage of episodes ending in collision
   $$\text{CR} = \frac{N_{\text{collision}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \times 100\%.$$

3. **Mean Final Distance (MFD):** Average Euclidean distance to goal at episode end
   $$\text{MFD} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{total}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{total}}}\|\mathbf{p}_T^{(j)} - \mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}^{(j)}\|.$$

4. **Root Mean Square Error (RMSE):** Time-averaged tracking error
   $$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\text{total}}T}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{total}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\mathbf{p}_t^{(j)} - \mathbf{w}_{\text{target},t}^{(j)}\|^2}.$$

5. **Episode Length (EL):** Mean number of steps to completion
   $$\text{EL} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{success}}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}T_j,$$
   where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of successful episodes.

6. **Cumulative Reward (CR):** Average total reward per episode
   $$\text{CR} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{total}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{total}}}\sum_{t=0}^{T_j}R_t^{(j)}.$$

### B. Baseline Methods

The baseline adopted in this study is a two-stage hybrid-intelligence unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) control framework, hereafter referred to as Baseline. Stage 1 provides low-level control: a reinforcement-learning agent--PPO, SAC, or TD3--is trained to hover accurately at an arbitrary target. Stage 2 performs high-level planning: a large language model (LLM) generates a sequence of intermediate way-points, while the Stage-1 agent executes them and performs on-line obstacle avoidance.

To validate the generality of Stage 1, an intra-class comparison is conducted among PPO+LLM, SAC+LLM, and TD3+LLM. To verify the superiority of Stage 2, an inter-class comparison is performed between PPO+LLM and the classical APF+PPO baseline (artificial potential field plus PPO, without LLM planning).

**Artificial Potential Field (APF) Baseline:** The APF method generates a control force as the gradient of a potential function:

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{att}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{obs}}}\mathbf{F}_{\text{rep},i},$$

where the attractive force toward the goal is:

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{att}} = -k_{\text{att}}(\mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}),$$

and the repulsive force from obstacle $i$ is:

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{rep},i} = \begin{cases}
k_{\text{rep}}\left(\frac{1}{d_i} - \frac{1}{d_0}\right)\frac{1}{d_i^2}\frac{\mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},i}}{d_i}, & d_i \leq d_0 \\
\mathbf{0}, & d_i > d_0
\end{cases}$$

where $d_i = \|\mathbf{p}_t - \mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},i}\|$, $k_{\text{att}}=1.0$, $k_{\text{rep}}=5.0$, and $d_0=1.5$ m is the influence radius. The resultant force is converted to a waypoint command: $\mathbf{p}_{\text{cmd}} = \mathbf{p}_t + 0.1\cdot\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}/\|\mathbf{F}_{\text{total}}\|$.

The full-component baseline used in ablation studies comprises:

- RL agent: PPO+LLM (or SAC+LLM, TD3+LLM)
- Complete geometric verification
- Virtual-target curriculum
- Full policy-diversity strategies

All performance gains and component contributions are reported relative to this full-configuration system.

### C. Test Environments

Three progressively challenging test environments were designed to evaluate robustness and generalization:

**Environment 1 (Env1) - Simple Configuration:**
- Obstacle 1: center $(3.0, 3.0, 1.0)$ m, radius $0.4$ m
- Obstacle 2: center $(6.0, 6.0, 1.5)$ m, radius $0.5$ m
- Start position: $(1.0, 1.0, 1.0)$ m
- Goal position: $(8.0, 8.0, 2.0)$ m
- Difficulty: Obstacles well-separated, single clear path

**Environment 2 (Env2) - Medium Configuration:**
- Obstacle 1: center $(4.5, 4.5, 1.2)$ m, radius $0.5$ m
- Obstacle 2: center $(5.5, 5.5, 1.8)$ m, radius $0.6$ m
- Start position: $(1.5, 1.5, 1.0)$ m
- Goal position: $(8.5, 8.5, 2.2)$ m
- Difficulty: Obstacles closer together, narrow passage

**Environment 3 (Env3) - Complex Configuration:**
- Obstacle 1: center $(5.0, 4.0, 1.5)$ m, radius $0.6$ m

- Goal position: $(8.0, 7.0, 2.5)$ m

The inter-obstacle clearance is computed as:

$$\Delta_{\text{clear}} = \|\mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},1} - \mathbf{p}_{\text{obs},2}\| - (r_{\text{obs},1} + r_{\text{obs},2}),$$

yielding $\Delta_{\text{clear}}^{\text{Env1}}=2.94$ m, $\Delta_{\text{clear}}^{\text{Env2}}=0.31$ m, $\Delta_{\text{clear}}^{\text{Env3}}=0.13$ m, demonstrating increasing difficulty.

## V CONCLUSION

We present a hybrid-intelligence control framework that decomposes aggressive quadrotor flight into two stages: low-level RL-based hovering and high-level LLM-based planning. Stage 1 adopts a curriculum-enhanced PPO agent with $\pm 50\%$ RPM authority, achieving $96.7\%$ success on 5-waypoint navigation. Stage 2 integrates an LLM planner with virtual targets, reducing the tracking RMSE to $0.08$ m while maintaining zero collision in dense obstacles. 

**Key Contributions:**

1. **Two-Stage Architecture:** Decoupling low-level control (RL) from high-level planning (LLM) enables modular design and independent optimization. The virtual target technology bridges the gap between global planning and local execution, achieving:
   $$\Delta_{\text{virtual}} = 94.0\% \text{ improvement over RL-only baseline.}$$

2. **Curriculum Learning Efficacy:** Progressive task complexity (1→2→5 waypoints) is essential for convergence, providing:
   $$\Delta_{\text{curriculum}} \geq 76.7\% \text{ improvement over direct training.}$$

3. **Action Space Design:** Larger control authority (±50% vs. ±5% RPM) directly translates to better maneuverability:
   $$\eta_{\text{control}} = \frac{\text{SR}_{\text{PPO}}}{\text{SR}_{\text{SAC}}} = \frac{96.7\%}{90.0\%} = 1.074.$$

4. **Robustness and Generalization:** Out-of-distribution testing confirms the framework maintains $\rho=0.941$ performance retention, significantly outperforming classical methods ($\rho_{\text{APF}}=0.750$).

**Limitations and Future Work:**

1. **LLM Planning Overhead:** While computationally efficient (<5 ms/step), LLM calls introduce latency. Future work should explore edge-optimized LLM models (e.g., quantized versions) for sub-millisecond planning.

2. **Dynamic Obstacles:** Current experiments focus on static obstacles. Extension to moving obstacles requires:
   - Velocity prediction: $\mathbf{p}_{\text{obs}}(t+\Delta t) = \mathbf{p}_{\text{obs}}(t) + \mathbf{v}_{\text{obs}}\Delta t$
   - Temporal CBF constraints: $\dot{h}(\mathbf{x},t) + \gamma h(\mathbf{x},t) \geq 0$

3. **Multi-Agent Coordination:** Scaling to swarm scenarios requires distributed consensus:
   $$\min_{\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_N}\sum_{i=1}^N J_i(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{u}_i) \text{ s.t. } \|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\| \geq d_{\text{min}}, \forall i\neq j.$$

4. **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** PyBullet simulations provide high-fidelity physics, but real-world deployment requires:
   - Domain randomization (mass, inertia, drag coefficients)
   - Visual servoing for perception (currently uses ground-truth state)
   - Hardware-in-the-loop testing on platforms like Crazyflie 2.1

5. **Safety Guarantees:** While CBF provides forward-invariance, formal verification tools (e.g., RAVEN, Verse) should certify the complete closed-loop system against adversarial perturbations.

In summary, this work demonstrates that hybrid intelligence—combining data-driven RL with model-based LLM reasoning—offers a promising paradigm for autonomous UAV navigation in complex, obstacle-rich environments. The proposed framework achieves state-of-the-art performance while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for real-time onboard deployment.
